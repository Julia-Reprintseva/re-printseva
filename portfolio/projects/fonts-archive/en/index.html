Story

I meant to delete it — it turned out to be an archive of an era

Sorting through an old drive, I found a folder called “Fonts”: 2,636 files, untouched since 2017. Back then I worked as a designer and typesetter at a print shop, and fonts were tools of the trade. My first thought was to delete the folder. Then I decided to look inside first. Opening them one by one — no thanks. I handed the folder to Claude Code, and it read the copyright notices of all 2,288 fonts.

Here is what came out. Nothing exclusive: these are the typical “Cyrillic fonts” bundles from CDs and torrents that thousands of designers have. But together they are a snapshot of the Russian type scene of the 1990s–2000s — the very letters used on cassette covers, newspapers and the first websites. The AI produced previews, a rough classification and attribution — and part of the attribution had to be restored: 138 ParaType fonts carried an Adobe copyright in their files, a trace of a pirate converter.

1,572 unique families; 1,858 with Cyrillic — 62 of which were pretending to be Latin: their Cyrillic is hidden in the Windows-1251 encoding, and the AI found it by comparing the drawings of the letters.

The catalogue

What it does in practice

Claude opened each of the 2,288 files and rendered a specimen — the same pangram for every font — 2,263 images in fifteen minutes. Then it assembled a page from them.

01

Find a font for the job in a minute, not an evening

Need a handwritten Cyrillic font for an invitation? Two clicks: type “handwritten”, tick “Cyrillic only”, 182 options as pictures. The filters depend on each other: pick “calligraphy” and only Sudtipos remains among the authors.

02

Know what font you are looking at

Click an author and a card appears: who, when, what studio. Fonts from films, games and brands say what they imitate. No torrent bundle has this.

03

Try your own text, not someone else’s phrase

Every font has a “Try” button. Type a company name, a headline, a line of verse — and see it in all the chosen fonts side by side, rendered from the real files. Assign one as the heading and another as body text, and a mini-page assembles below. Neither Word nor Font Book can do this.

04

Stay out of court and don’t be fooled

Every font carries a licence tag, and there is a list of free replacements for client work. 62 “disguised” Cyrillic fonts were found; 24 symbol fonts are honestly marked “no specimen”, 19 as “capitals only”. An honest “don’t know” beats pretty gibberish.

In the public version only the free fonts (232) can be tried on. Commercial font files are not published on the site — that would amount to distributing them; they carry a “try locally” tag instead. Specimen images are shown for all 2,288. The catalogue interface is in Russian.

The skill

The warehouse became a pipeline

A year ago I struggled with ChatGPT: I was uploading scanned primers for “Letters of the 20th Century”, and the uploads silently failed — it turned out to be an undocumented 80 MB limit for the whole account. Every task had to start from scratch. I wrote about it in detail.

Now I work differently: I build skills in Claude. A skill is an instruction the AI reads by itself in any project: where things are, how to search, what is allowed and what is not. This case produced a skill that typesets text with font pairings. The value is bigger than the archive itself: an archive is a warehouse, and value appears when the warehouse is connected to a pipeline. “Typeset this invitation in a 1990s mood, heading in Type Market, body in a text face with Cyrillic” — and a minute later there is a finished page where everything is visible at once.

Can AI pair fonts like a professional designer? Like a good craftsman — yes; like an outstanding designer — no. Pairing is 80% rules: no more than two or three fonts; contrast in character; one era or one mood; one role per font. The remaining 20% is taste. The AI covers the first 80% and shows the options as pictures; the decision stays with a human.

The same sourdough bread guide, typeset in three pairings from the collection. I said “show me three options” — and pointed at one.
