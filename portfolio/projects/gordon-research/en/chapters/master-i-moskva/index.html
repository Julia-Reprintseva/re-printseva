Task

Find every quotation involving Moscow topography in “The Master and Margarita” and “Theatrical Novel”, collect Bulgakov’s places from scholarly research, and all of Mandelstam’s Moscow place names; then check the finished research and the theory of “Mandelstam as the prototype of the Master”.

What was done

Three research runs and a separate fact-checking pass: Ivan Bezdomny’s route was checked against a 1930s map of Moscow, the addresses against the writer’s biography; the Mandelstam theory was taken apart source by source. Where AI failed — in Mandelstam’s Moscow place names — was recorded separately.

The topography of Moscow in Bulgakov and Mandelstam (Gemini)

Project document · 8 October 2025

I. Introduction: Moscow as a chronotope of literary confrontation

Bulgakov uses topography as a precise anchor for satire and for mystifying Soviet everyday life; Mandelstam uses it to record and preserve personal and cultural memory, resisting the politics of oblivion. Methodology: comparing the locus realis (verified addresses) with the locus poeticus (the artistic image). The challenge is the asymmetry of the data: Bulgakov’s topography is well documented, while Mandelstam’s lacks verified addresses.

II. Cartographic dynamics of the 1920s–1930s

The politics of renaming as an instrument of ideology. Mass replacement of pre-revolutionary names; centralisation through the Mossovet; the chaos of duplicate names; in 1932 the Mossovet and the Architectural Planning Department (APU) tried to bring order to the process. For Bulgakov — a tool for highlighting the absurdity of bureaucracy; for Mandelstam — an act of erasing cultural heritage.

The geography of the literary apparatus. MASSOLIT (Griboyedov House) — the Gorky Literary Institute, Tverskoy Boulevard, 25 (a parody of MAPP and RAPP). The Dramlit House — Lavrushinsky Lane, 17. The literary nomenklatura inhabits a space that once belonged to the bourgeoisie and the nobility.

III. Bulgakov: the topography of prototypes

  1. Patriarch’s Ponds (Malaya Bronnaya) — the Patriarch’s Sloboda, residences of the clergy — the scene of an atheist debate and of infernal intervention.
  2. Bolshaya Sadovaya, 10, flat 50 — Bulgakov’s home in 1921–1924, turned into the “bad flat”: in the era of forced flat-sharing, the home lost its function as protection.
  3. MASSOLIT (Tverskoy, 25) and the Variety (Triumfalnaya Square, 2 — the Satire Theatre) — centres of corrupt art, punished by an explosion and a witches’ sabbath.
  4. Residences of grandeur: Pashkov House (Vozdvizhenka, 3/5) — Woland’s final flight; Margarita’s mansion — the Foreign Ministry reception house on Spiridonovka, 17 (Shekhtel, interiors by Vrubel). The true “self” of Moscow survives in pre-Soviet buildings.

“Theatrical Novel”: the Independent Theatre — the Moscow Art Theatre, Kamergersky Lane, 3; the closed, stuffy space of the wings versus the chaotic space of “The Master”.

IV. Mandelstam: Moscow as a chronotope of memory

Temporary flats (Nashchokinsky and Furmanny lanes) — the status of an outcast poet. Architectural details (the Kremlin, cathedrals) — anchors of memory, proof that culture cannot be destroyed. Mandelstam’s Moscow is a palimpsest.

V. Comparative toponymy

  • Bulgakov’s precision vs Mandelstam’s diffusion: addresses as evidence (Spiridonovka, 17; Lavrushinsky, 17) versus figurative names.
  • Satire versus elegy: Bulgakov’s Moscow dies of bureaucracy and is resurrected through fire; Mandelstam’s Moscow resists oblivion.
  • Geographic radius: Bulgakov — centres of the elite (Garden Ring, Tverskaya); Mandelstam — forced isolation.

VII. Conclusion

Bulgakov’s Moscow is a mystical prototype: precise addresses as a hyperrealistic backdrop for fantastical destruction; salvation is tied to pre-Soviet gems. Mandelstam’s is an epic testimony: a space of memory against the politics of erasure. Both authors created a “Moscow text” as a myth of a city resisting ideological uniformity.

1. Ivan Bezdomny’s route: correspondence to the real topography of Moscow

The research confirms that the scene of Ivan Bezdomny chasing Woland fully corresponds to the real topography of Moscow in the 1930s.

The route in the text

  1. Patriarch’s Ponds — Ivan is sitting on a bench with Berlioz when Woland appears.
  2. Bolshoy Patriarshy Lane — Woland, Koroviev and Behemoth disappear into the lane.
  3. Spiridonovka — the characters run out to the Nikitsky Gates.
  4. A bus to Arbat Square — Koroviev jumps onto a bus.
  5. Behemoth on the “A” tram — the cat jumps onto the footboard of the “Annushka”.
  6. Arbat, Prechistenka (Kropotkinskaya St.), Ostozhenka — Ivan runs through courtyards and loses the trail.
  7. Pozharsky Lane — he bursts into a stranger’s communal flat (probable prototype — No. 12, flat 66).the lane is not named in the novel: it is a prototype found by local historians, not a place name from the text
  8. Prechistenskaya Embankment / the Moskva River — Ivan jumps into the river.

Confirmation from sources

  • The route has been reconstructed by Bulgakov scholars and local historians (map by Valeri Leonov: leonovvaleri.livejournal.com/26549.html).the route details — Pozharsky Lane 12, flat 66, the stairs to the water, the Babiy Gorodok dam — come from a different post: in-moskow.livejournal.com/3098 “The Chase after Woland”
  • All the streets and points correspond to places that existed.
  • The spot where he jumps into the river is the Prechistenskaya Embankment opposite Savvinsky Lanecorrect: opposite Pozharsky (then Savelyevsky) Lane; in the 1930s there was a double staircase down to the water there, and Bulgakov himself swam at this spot.

Conclusion: the route fully corresponds to historical Moscow. No errors.the report itself did contain errors — see the notes above

2. Bulgakov and Moscow: how the place names match the writer’s biography

Where Bulgakov lived (in fact and in the novel)

  1. Bolshaya Sadovaya, No. 10, flat 50 — lived there in 1921–1924; encoded as building No. 302-bis, flat 50 — the “bad flat”. Now a museum. Confirmed 100%.
  2. Nashchokinsky Lane, No. 3/5, flat 6 — last place of residence, with Yelena Shilovskaya (1936–1940).correct: from 1934 — the report itself names 1934 below Confirmed by archives and by Yelena Bulgakova’s memoirs.
  3. Mansurovsky Lane, No. 9 (at his friend Yermolinsky’s) — prototype of the Master’s basement flat: a quiet courtyard, a stove, lilac. Confirmed by local historians and by letters.
  4. Povarskaya Street — prototype of “Griboyedov House”, probably Povarskaya 52 (the Central House of Writers). Cited by Myagkov and Selemeneva.

Prototypes of locations: friends’ addresses

  1. Pozharsky Lane, No. 12, flat 66 — where Ivan Bezdomny bursts in; the house of Bulgakov’s acquaintances.
  2. Sivtsev Vrazhek, No. 26 — the Kreshkov family house; characters of “Theatrical Novel” were modelled on its residents (monograph by N. Katayeva).
  3. Merzlyakovsky and Filippovsky lanes, the Arbat courtyards — walks in drafts and essays.

Conclusion

The research accurately identifies the real addresses behind the prototypes. “Griboyedov House” → Herzen House / House of Writers; “Stravinsky’s clinic” → Krasnushkin’s clinic in the Sokol area; “the Variety” → the theatre in the Aquarium garden or the Estrada Theatre on Sadovaya. No errors found; the prototype of the Master’s basement was pinned down — Mansurovsky 9.

3. Is Osip Mandelstam the prototype of the Master?

What scholars say

  • Most Bulgakov scholars (Chudakova, Myagkov, Lebedev): the basis of the Master is Bulgakov himself (a rejected writer; burns his manuscript; the basement; autobiographical intonations).
  • Some scholars point to similarities with Mandelstam. Boris Gasparov: “the image of the Master in many ways recalls Mandelstam’s fate”not a quotation: Boris Gasparov has no direct “Master = Mandelstam” formula; he analyses Mandelstam motifs in the novel — “Iz nablyudeniy nad motivnoy strukturoy romana M. A. Bulgakova ‘Master i Margarita’”, Slavica Hierosolymitana III, 1978 — arrest and exile; mental breakdown (Mandelstam jumped from a hospital window during his exile in Voronezhcorrect: in Cherdyn, 1934); the role of the wife (Nadezhda Mandelstam — like Margarita); the feeling of being a “cast-out poet”.
  • Coincidences of fate: Bulgakov and Mandelstam lived in the same building in 1934 — Nashchokinsky Lane, 3/5; Bulgakov witnessed the arrestnot confirmed by sources; Bulgakov’s wife helped Nadezhda Mandelstam financially; the figure of the Master appears in the drafts soon after these events.

What Bulgakov’s texts say

There are no direct allusions to Mandelstam in the novel. The Master’s name is never given — a pointer to a self-portrait. Bulgakov signed himself “M. Neznakomets” (“M. Stranger”).

Conclusion

There is no direct evidence. The biographical overlap makes an influence possible, if partial. Echoes of Mandelstam’s fate in the Master — but not a full prototype.
